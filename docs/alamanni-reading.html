Alamanni to Piero de' Medici, 26 April 1494

A letter of Piero Alamanni, Florentine orator at Lodovico Sforza's court, to Piero de' Medici, Vigevano, 26 April 1494 (ASF, Mediceo avanti il Principato LXXIV doc. 96), with cipher passages urging the Neapolitan stroke against Genoa. Read with the same key sheet as Ridolfi's letters, MAP LXXII 183.

Piero Alamanni, Florentine orator at Lodovico Sforza's court, to Piero di Lorenzo de' Medici, Archivio di Stato di Firenze, Mediceo avanti il Principato (MAP) LXXIV doc. 96, cc. 220-221. Clear letter with cipher passages on 220v, 221r and 221v. Key: the Milan embassy key sheet MAP LXXII 183, the same key Ridolfi used in October 1494 (see README.md and key.json).

This is a partial reading: 82 of the 91 cipher words counted in alamanni-transcription.tsv have a proposed reading (two of them doubtful), 9 are unread, and the word count is itself provisional where the word division of an unread run is unknown. More than half the words are free of a choice between sign values, now that the ÷ sign is told apart as n or c by its dots (14 November 2026; see [held-out-114](held-out-114/README.md)). The best-supported parts are the words graded H or C in reading.txt, whose sign values come from the key sheet or a contemporary gloss; a sign can still have been misread.

How to read the text below:

  • Cipher passages are in italics; everything else is the clear hand. la salute before del re Alfonso is clear text, not cipher.
  • Spelling of the cipher passages is normalized for u/v and for doubled consonants. Letters that no cipher sign stands for are in [brackets] (n[ost]ra, travagl[i]o, [in]dugarlo, where the "in" is taken from a preceding m-like mark). [?] is an unread group or sign; (?) marks a doubtful reading, in the cipher or in the clear hand.
  • Name number 22. The key sheet gives 22 as "S. di Pesaro" (and S. Lodovico as 15). Ridolfi's letter LXXIV 107, c. 241r, in the same key, has the contemporary gloss "S. Lco" written over a 22, and the three passages here concern Lodovico's intentions. So 22 is probably Lodovico in this correspondence, but that rests on one gloss in another man's letter: it is rendered [22: probably S. Lodovico; cf. Ridolfi 107 gloss; sheet: S. di Pesaro] at the first occurrence and [22] after.

[220r] Magnifico Piero. Io ho veduto quanto tu mi di' per la lettera tua da parte: et ti risponderò brevemente. Sappi in prima, che io non ho preso dispiacere alchuno della repositione di messer Guido et Piero: né ho mai improbato in parte alchuna la deliberatione vostra etc. Solo ero un poco differente nel modo: affine di potere almeno venire a quell'altra parte etc. Quanto al protesto, che si lasciò indrieto: mi dispiacerebbe, che ti fussi stato suggesto, che vi fussi intervenuto mio consiglio: perché come per l'altra ti dixi, io non vidi(?) le commissioni, senò poi: et non accadde(?) il farlo. Pure anche non me turbo niente: et sono contento, che se fussi venuto ben ad altri(?) di porre questo peso adosso a me, di comportarlo: perché credo mi [...] fede della verità: et questo importa poco. [L'oratore ... haveva](?) havuto buono ordine: et di poi è venuto(?) di fare il protesto come vedrai per l'alligata mia [...]. Et io l'ho facto et replicato più volte et in presentia di testimoni: et puncto(?) costui con tali parole per rimuoverlo, che harei creduto in altro tempo farlo saltare. Nondimancho vedi, che non s'è rimutato. Et di questo ne lascerò fare la chiosa costì a voi. Et del discorso, che tu di' havere facto el Conte di Caiazo a vostri(?) imbasciadori, non essendo informato, non te posso rispondere.

Circa la sicurtà da darsi a costui: non mi accade dire altro: perché vedi, che habbiamo [...].

English (cipher passages in italics)

Magnificent Piero. I have seen what you tell me in your separate letter, and will answer briefly. Know first that I took no offence at the recall(?) of messer Guido and Piero, nor did I ever disapprove your decision; I only differed a little on the manner [...]. As to the protest that was left aside, I should be sorry if it had been suggested to you that my advice entered into it [...]. [220r, remainder: he made and repeated the protest before witnesses; "this man" was not moved; on the Count of Caiazzo's discourse he cannot answer; on the surety to be given, nothing more to say.]

You will see, my Piero, from the enclosed what twistings these are of Signor Lodovico, and lately what effects begin to show. This morning he told the king's [Neapolitan] envoys the same about the galleys being made at Genoa [...]. So that, putting together all these movements of his, and seeing the provision he makes at Genoa for the fleet, [I do not] know what to say. And considering the chapter you send me from Naples of what the King says about the matter of turning Genoa [...], I am confirmed - though in my last I was a little restrained - that this would be the one medicine for the cure of all this trouble; and that [[22, probably Signor Lodovico] followed by three unread groups] ... to repair all the harms, this is the one and only thing. But that anyone should be for delaying it [I do not hold], because perhaps later one will want to do it in time and will not be able. [...] But I would remind you with affection that to enter upon a thing of this nature requires going into it with such preparations, and foreseeing it so far won, that one went(?) to manifest victory; and I should much like that you [here?] should(?) have to come into the open for every good reason - it would also better serve the mind of the King [clear word after 'del re' not read: begins 'Fe/Fr'], that is, of Naples, in any event - but rather that under cover you should give them what help you could [...]. While Genoa stays as it is now, [...] and in sum, if you could be in time, or saw a way to do it cleanly (ferla and netta uncertain), I believe it would be the salvation of King Alfonso and ours. And the hope they have at Naples of seeing clearly the disposition of [22] [...] seems to me superfluous, [...]; and do not believe that [22], even if he had more security than is possible, will ever trust the King, [...] [nor] wants such greatness of his, judging it not to his own purpose, nor to yours there. And there is no doubt that to arrive at this end he will try everything. And if he gets a footing with the fleet [one sign unread] Genoa, see where things will slide. Nevertheless I leave everything to your opinion and judgment [...]. I do beg you earnestly not to speak of this opinion of mine to a living soul for now, because I would not want it laid to my charge that I wanted war, to put the city in turmoil, as you know happened to me once before [...].

[...] And in the end he wanted to show us three things that lie in his power to be able to [three groups not read; the sense wants something like 'harm us']: the first, of Genoa; the other, of France, which [...] long held [one group not read] will bring it down on us(?) [one sign not read]; the third, to close to us the road of the merchants in the West, France, Spain and such places, believing by these means, or by making us suspect [...], that discontent may arise in the [city] that could have some bad effect. [...] Farewell. Vigevano, 26 April 1494. Piero Alamanni, knight etc.
