# Roberto Acciaiuoli at Amiens, 23 October 1513

**Read at about the 95% word bar, 20 November 2026; not yet reviewed by a second model.** Of the 1,890 cipher signs on the two faces, 1,716 (91%) are read as running Italian. By words, about 710 of some 780 are read outright and about 33 more are read with a correction I supply, which together is about 95%. About 40 words are doubtful or unread. Louis XII has ordered Monsieur de Bourbon into Burgundy with lances and Landsknechte. A copy of a Martelli letter, reporting the rout of a party written as 15, has overturned a plan that Acciaiuoli had been discussing. He reports a long conversation with the king, who is torn between fear of the Swiss and anger at people who excuse their conduct by fear of the English army. He follows the English king's embarkation at Calais, counts the lances and Landsknechte on the French side, quotes the king's message that he is secure and will fight if the Swiss move, and ends with the prelates leaving for Rome after All Saints and the king's wish to satisfy the pope. Two code numbers, 15 and 22, are identified from context only, and one name is unread.

The manuscript is Florence, Archivio di Stato, Mediceo avanti il Principato, filza LXXII, doc. 192, old c. 234. The archive's images are 1272 × 1688 pixels, its native maximum. The heading is plural, *Magnifici viri*, and the subscription names Robertus Acciarolus. The cipher occupies 22 lines on the recto and 19 on the verso, the last ending where ordinary handwriting resumes. ## What is still open

- **Unread signs.** 174 signs sit in groups that decode to letters but not to words I will stand behind. For most of them I give the word I take them for in brackets, with a question mark where it is a guess. What has no gloss comes to about 28 words: part of R5, the name after *contro a* at R12, the start of the news clause at V1, a few words at V5 and V11, *tucti li …* at V13, and the phrase before *buon riposo* at V14–15.
- **What the plan was.** R3–7 turns on three words I supply, *passaggio*, *costà* and *successo*. If they are right, Acciaiuoli is describing a French passage over the Alps that the rout of 15 has now made impossible. The signs as transcribed do not spell those words.
- **Code 15.** The party whose rout upset the plan and whose backing is mentioned at R6. The Venetians, routed near Vicenza on 7 October 1513, fit both places, and the Martelli named here was Florence's man on that front (see the documentary checks). This rests on context. In the sibling letter 190 a number read as 15 stands where the clear copy has *Rubertet* written over a struck word, beside 51 for the same man, so 15 there may be a slip for 51 or a different use. That conflict is unresolved.
- **Who the king is angry at.** R12 has *contro a* and then three signs that I first took for the number 26. All three blind readers saw separate signs there, so I no longer read a code. The speech that follows fits subjects who gave way to the English, and Sanudo reports that the people of Tournai sent to the king to explain their conduct. The name itself is unread.
- **Code 22.** The troops counted in thousands. Dudout tentatively reads *lanzichenech*, and Sanudo's report of the same order calls the infantry Landsknechte.
- ***Monsignore*** in the exchange about excuses is not named.
- **Word signs** R with a small circle (R10; *quello* fits), B with a raised 1 (V15, V16), a large V (V15), a capital-H-like sign (R6), an F with a mark above (V5; *essere* fits, as F with a raised circle is in the 190/191 pair) and a large G with a raised tick (R10, R12, V9; an adverb of time such as *hieri* fits all three) have no fixed value.
- **Two circle signs.** The 190/191 pair has a slashed circle for **t** (*ristrignere*, *tractar*) beside a phi with a long descender for **x** (*dixe*). I transcribed both as one barred circle, X, read x. That is why *dis[t]ribuito*, *al[t]ra* and *riscon[t]ro* come out with x before r and are left as glosses. Separating the two shapes on the page would turn those into plain readings and should be part of the second review.
- **Numerals.** 8, 10, 12, 14 and the 2 and 5 on V6 are read as figures because *cento* or *mila* follows. An unmarked 8 is elsewhere the letter t, and 2 and 5 are elsewhere o and m.

## How it was read

Brice Dudout reconstructed a Florentine syllabary for Francesco Pandolfini's letters from the French court in 1514–15. A consonant sign takes its vowel from a superscript figure, 1–5 for **u, o, i, e, a**. There are bare vowel and consonant signs, doubled consonants, whole-word signs, and numbers used as code words. Acciaiuoli's letter of a year earlier uses the same system. [Dudout's key and source pairs](https://github.com/dbourdeau/cyphersolver/tree/main/targets/pandolfini1514)

[key.tsv](key.tsv) lists every value used here and where it comes from. Rows marked `v6` are Dudout's values for the shape his legend describes. Rows marked `190/191` are fixed by known plaintext in this hand, described below: a closed-loop y for **o**, a pi-like sign for **p**, a barred long s for **ss**, a tall b-like sign for **i**, the beta-like sign for **e** and the barred circle for **x** (Dudout's legend gives the barred circle as e), and the word signs *vostra*, *nostro*, *tempo*, *chi* and, for a triangle standing alone, *bene*. Rows marked `192` rest on sense within this letter alone: a tall 3 with an ascender for **a**, a g-like sign for **z**, a plain cross for **n**, an E-like sign for **e**, and the word sign *se*. The 2-like sign is c by default and b by sense, as in Dudout's material. The twenty-three places where I read b, a numeral or another value against the sign as written are listed one by one in [192-repairs.tsv](192-repairs.tsv).

Two internal checks do not depend on the sense of a single passage. The sign sequence for *distribui-* occurs with two endings, *distribuire le gvernigione* (R2) and *dis[t]ribuito el passag[i]o loro* (V4). *Gvernigione* recurs at R22, where the troops assigned at R2 have still not left.

**Known plaintext in the same hand.** Doc. 190 is a letter of the same day to Giuliano de' Medici, almost all in cipher, and doc. 191 is its contemporary copy in clear. A separate Claude instance transcribed 190 from the image, read the clear copy, and aligned the two sign by sign ([190-calibration.md](190-calibration.md), [190-alignment.tsv](190-alignment.tsv)). It fitted corrections to Dudout's key on the first nine cipher lines and scored the rest. On the 661 held-out signs the corrected key gives the clear's letters at 92%. Most of those face clear words that were only recognised once the cipher suggested them, so that figure is partly circular. On the 93 held-out signs whose clear word was read before any decoding, the corrected key agrees at 84% taking the first alternative at doubtful signs and 94% taking the best. Its report puts most of the remaining errors down to confusions of similar shapes and shifted superscripts. The values that pair fixes are the `190/191` rows of the key. The calibration used its own names for the shapes, and I matched them to mine by description, not by setting crops side by side. The clear copy itself is a hard hand at this resolution and its reading has not been checked by anyone else. ## How far the transcription can be trusted

I transcribed both faces from enlarged line strips with the key in hand. That first pass is kept as [192-first-reading.txt](192-first-reading.txt). It was not hashed before decoding, and the file was retyped from the session record after the working copy was deleted, so its being untouched rests on my word. [192-transcription.txt](192-transcription.txt) differs from it at 195 signs. Those changes are of three kinds: a sign re-read on a tighter crop of that sign, a shape given its own name (the closed-loop y, plain numerals), and places where blind readers gave a sign I had read differently or not at all, and I adopted theirs. In the first round that was about 27 signs in blotted stretches. In the second round it was every sign on thirteen lines that both readers gave alike. A change made only because a word needed it is not in the transcription. It is a row of the repairs file, or the word is left as an unread group with my gloss beside it.

Two fresh readers transcribed the same strips from a legend of shape names, with no key, no values and no other file ([blind/](blind/)). Decoded with the same key, their signs give the same letters as mine at 1,499 of the 1,716 signs I count as read (87%), and at 1,554 (91%) if three consistent differences of naming are allowed for, such as their *d* for the sigma-like bare s. The count includes the 27 or so signs I adopted from these passes, which agree by construction.

A second round went back to the thirteen lines that held the unread clauses (R4–6, R10, R12, V1, V4–5, V8–10, V15–16), with tighter crops and a legend that spelled out how superscript 1 differs from 5 ([blind2/](blind2/)). Two more fresh readers worked separately, one in reverse line order. Where both gave the same sign and it differed from mine, I took theirs. That is what opened V8–10, *venne nuova a .4. come quella Maestà* at V1, *le [co]se de[l] Re secure* at V5, *[f]ranzese* at R6 and *de' quali [mi parlò]* at R12. Readings resting on that agreement have two blind witnesses and no key-aware one, which is the opposite footing from the rest of the letter. The remaining tenth is mostly superscripts, above all 5 read as 1, which changes a vowel. Where the two disagree, my reading rests on the word, and that is a weaker footing than the page. The blind passes independently give *Martelli*, *Cales*, *Borbona*, *Tucti Sancti*, *credo*, *gratia*, *fine desiderato* and the figures 8, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 20 and 22. They do not give 26.

## Documentary checks

Marino Sanudo's *Diarii*, vol. XVII, cols. 321–324, records a separate report of the king assigning Bourbon to Burgundy with **900 men-at-arms and 10,000 infantry**, later called Landsknechte. This confirms the commander, the destination and the infantry. The letter's 8 hundred lances and Sanudo's 900 men-at-arms are different figures and I have not reconciled them. Another report in the same volume gives 1,500 lances and 15,000 Landsknechte. The same volume prints a Florentine letter of 28 August 1513 that cites reports from *nostro Piero Martelli* at Vicenza, with the Spanish army then facing the Venetians (index: Martelli Pietro, cols. 25–26). The sign before *Martelli* at R7 would be his initial. A Martelli letter from that front reporting a rout, forwarded from Florence on 11 October, fits the Venetian defeat near Vicenza on 7 October, which Sanudo's volume covers at length around Creazzo and Olmo. This is the documentary ground for reading 15 as the Venetians. The volume also records that news of a defeat changed French plans to send troops to Italy, that the people of Tournai had sent to the French king to explain their conduct, and that prelates were to be sent to Rome. [Sanudo, vol. XVII](https://archive.org/details/idiariidimarinos17sanu)

Desjardins and Canestrini, *Négociations diplomatiques de la France avec la Toscane*, vol. II, print Acciaiuoli's dispatch from Amiens of 26 September 1513 from this filza. The next letter of his in the edition is from Blois, 27 January 1514. The letter of 23 October is not there. [Desjardins, vol. II](https://archive.org/details/gri_33125010469852)

The photographic index to Gabbrielli's historical compilation lists **no. 120, Acciaioli Roberto, 1512, 1513, 1514**. The online Yale reel has volume I, with image files through 58-51 and three index images. The entry names a surviving key to compare. I have not obtained its table. It is the way to fix codes 15 and 22 and the open word signs. [Gabbrielli's index, image 2](https://dataverse.yale.edu/api/access/datafile/7983) · [Yale reel inventory](https://doi.org/10.60600/YU/XKVOWE)

## Grades

Of the 1,716 signs read, 1,416 are read by Dudout's values from the sign as transcribed, and 136 by values fixed on the 190/191 pair. Dudout calls his key partial and unmeasured. Measured here on doc. 190 against its clear copy, it gives the clear's letters at 85% of signs before any correction. On that footing these are grade C under the repository's conventions, with the caution above about how the clear copy was read. A further 127 are read by values that rest on sense within this letter. The conventions have no grade for that short of S, which needs a control I have not run, so they are reported as their own count. Fourteen are read at a sign marked doubtful (M) and 23 after a declared repair (I). The 174 unread signs are M.

By words, counted the way the Cyphersolver readings of the Pandolfini letters are (certain and probable words against the total): about 710 words are read outright, about 33 are read with a letter or word I correct in brackets, about 12 carry a question mark, and about 28 are unread, out of some 780. That puts the reading at about 95%, with an error of a point or two either way because unread groups do not map exactly onto words. Everything in square brackets is I.

## Reproducing the reading

From the repository root:

```sh
python3 amiens-1513/decode.py
python3 amiens-1513/verify.py
python3 amiens-1513/blind_compare.py
```

The first prints the literal decode of the 41 manuscript lines from the transcription, the key and the repairs. The second checks that [192-decode.txt](192-decode.txt) and every row of [192-reading.txt](192-reading.txt) follow from them letter for letter, recounts the signs by how they are read, counts the signs changed since the first reading, and exits non-zero if a committed file is stale. The third recomputes the agreement with the blind passes. These check derivation. Whether a sign on the page is the sign in the transcription has to be checked against the manuscript images, which are not redistributed here.

Andrew Aymeloglu's research project. OpenAI Codex made the first transcription and identified the key and the Bourbon passage. Claude re-transcribed both faces from the images, extended the key and made the reading above. Five further Claude instances made the four blind passes and the 190/191 calibration. No model other than Claude has reviewed this reading.
