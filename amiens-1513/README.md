# Roberto Acciaiuoli at Amiens, 23 October 1513

**Partial: substantially deciphered, with a continuous proposed reading, 20 November 2026.** Of the 1,887 cipher signs on the two faces, 1,730 (92%) are accepted into the reading. By words, about 710 of some 785 are read from the signs with nothing supplied (90%); about 50 more are read only with a letter or word that I supply in brackets, and about 22 are unread. That is short of the 95% of sure words that Cyphersolver asks before a document counts as read. Louis XII has ordered Monsieur de Bourbon into Burgundy with lances and Landsknechte. A copy of a Martelli letter, reporting the rout of a party written as 15, has overturned a plan that Acciaiuoli had been discussing. He reports a long conversation with the king, who is torn between fear of the Swiss and anger at people who excuse their conduct by fear of the English army. He follows the English king's embarkation at Calais, counts the lances and Landsknechte on the French side, quotes the king's message that he is secure and will fight if the Swiss move, and ends with the prelates leaving for Rome after All Saints and the king's wish to satisfy the pope. Acciaiuoli's own key survives in a nineteenth-century reconstruction, Gabbrielli's no. 120, and it confirms the sign values and the numbers 2, 4, 6, 20 and 22. Codex reviewed the reading against the images on 20 November 2026 ([codex-review.md](codex-review.md)); the findings I could confirm on the page are applied here.

The manuscript is Florence, Archivio di Stato, Mediceo avanti il Principato, filza LXXII, doc. 192, old c. 234. The archive's images are 1272 × 1688 pixels, its native maximum. The heading is plural, *Magnifici viri*, and the subscription names Robertus Acciarolus. The cipher occupies 22 lines on the recto and 19 on the verso, the last ending where ordinary handwriting resumes. ## What is still open

- **Unread signs.** 157 signs sit in groups that decode to letters but not to words I will stand behind. For most of them I give the word I take them for in brackets, with a question mark where it is a guess. What has no gloss comes to about 20 words: part of R5, the name after *contro a* at R12, the start of the news clause at V1, a few words at V5 and V11, *tucti li …* at V13, and the phrase before *buon riposo* at V14–15.
- **What the plan was.** *Passaggio* is spelled out twice, here and at V4, with the key's double-g sign. Its direction and outcome rest on two words I supply with a query, *costà* and *successo*. If they are right, Acciaiuoli is describing a French passage over the Alps that the rout of 15 has made impossible.
- **Code 15.** The party whose rout upset the plan and whose backing is mentioned at R6. The Venetians, routed near Vicenza on 7 October 1513, fit both places, and the Martelli named here was Florence's man on that front (see the documentary checks). Key 120 has no 15. This rests on context. In the sibling letter 190 a number read as 15 stands where the clear copy has *Rubertet* written over a struck word, beside 51 for the same man, so 15 there may be a slip for 51 or a different use. That conflict is unresolved.
- **Who the king is angry at.** After *contro a* R12 has a 2, a 6, the sign for *et*, a 4 and a 7. Codex reads this as two code numbers joined by *et*, 26 and 47, which gives the plural *de' quali* its antecedents. Neither number is in key 120. In the later Pandolfini letters 26 is the archduke. Sanudo reports that the people of Tournai sent to the king to explain their conduct. The identification is open.
- **Key 120 against this letter.** The key has no entry for the plain cross I read as n, the E-like sign I read as e, or the word sign *vostra*. It distinguishes shapes that my transcription merges: a round 2 for b from an angular one for c, a low sigma for s from the tall 6 used for p, and a barred 9 for z from the plain 9 for v. Where the reading turns on one of those, the choice was made by the word. Matching Gabbrielli's nineteenth-century drawings to Acciaiuoli's hand is itself a judgement.
- **The king's message.** Codex suggests the two signs after *scrivete* at V9 may be *a .2.*, "write to the pope". I could not confirm that and leave them unread.
- ***Monsignore*** in the exchange about excuses is not named.
- **Word signs.** B with a raised 1 (V15, V16) and an F with a mark above (R13, V5; the key has F with a raised circle for *effetto*, and *essere* fits both places) have no fixed value. The sign I read as *ora* (R10, R12, V9) is the key's G with a raised tick that the key does not show, so that value is a proposal.
- **Two circle signs.** The 190/191 pair has a slashed circle for **t** (*ristrignere*, *tractar*) beside a phi with a long descender for **x** (*dixe*). I transcribed both as one barred circle, X, read x. That is why *dis[t]ribuito*, *al[t]ra* and *riscon[t]ro* come out with x before r and are left as glosses. Separating the two shapes on the page would turn those into plain readings and should be part of the second review.
- **Numerals.** 8, 10, 12, 14 and the 2 and 5 on V6 are read as figures because *cento* or *mila* follows. An unmarked 8 is elsewhere the letter t, and 2 and 5 are elsewhere o and m.

## How it was read

Brice Dudout reconstructed a Florentine syllabary for Francesco Pandolfini's letters from the French court in 1514–15. A consonant sign takes its vowel from a superscript figure, 1–5 for **u, o, i, e, a**. There are bare vowel and consonant signs, doubled consonants, whole-word signs, and numbers used as code words. Acciaiuoli's letter of a year earlier uses the same system. [Dudout's key and source pairs](https://github.com/dbourdeau/cyphersolver/tree/main/targets/pandolfini1514)

[key.tsv](key.tsv) lists every value used here and where it comes from. Rows marked `G120` stand in Acciaiuoli's own key as Gabbrielli reconstructed it (see the documentary checks); that is now nearly all of them. Rows marked `v6` are Dudout's values with no counterpart there. Rows marked `190/191` are fixed by known plaintext in this hand, described below, and rows marked `192` rest on sense within this letter alone: a plain cross for **n**, an E-like sign for **e**. The key has two different 2-like signs for b and c, which I transcribed as one, c by default and b by sense. The twenty-three places where I read b, a numeral or another value against the sign as written are listed one by one in [192-repairs.tsv](192-repairs.tsv).

Two internal checks do not depend on the sense of a single passage. The sign sequence for *distribui-* occurs with two endings, *distribuire le gvernigione* (R2) and *dis[t]ribuito el passag[i]o loro* (V4). *Gvernigione* recurs at R22, where the troops assigned at R2 have still not left.

**Known plaintext in the same hand.** Doc. 190 is a letter of the same day to Giuliano de' Medici, almost all in cipher, and doc. 191 is its contemporary copy in clear. A separate Claude instance transcribed 190 from the image, read the clear copy, and aligned the two sign by sign ([190-calibration.md](190-calibration.md), [190-alignment.tsv](190-alignment.tsv)). It fitted corrections to Dudout's key on the first nine cipher lines and scored the rest. On the 661 held-out signs the corrected key gives the clear's letters at 92%. Most of those face clear words that were only recognised once the cipher suggested them, so that figure is partly circular. On the 93 held-out signs whose clear word was read before any decoding, the corrected key agrees at 84% taking the first alternative at doubtful signs and 94% taking the best. Its report puts most of the remaining errors down to confusions of similar shapes and shifted superscripts. The values that pair fixes are the `190/191` rows of the key. The calibration used its own names for the shapes, and I matched them to mine by description, not by setting crops side by side. The clear copy itself is a hard hand at this resolution and its reading has not been checked by anyone else. [Doc. 190r](https://archiviodistatofirenze.cultura.gov.it/map/riproduzione/?id=79570) · [Doc. 191r](https://archiviodistatofirenze.cultura.gov.it/map/riproduzione/?id=79572)

## How far the transcription can be trusted

I transcribed both faces from enlarged line strips with the key in hand. That first pass is kept as [192-first-reading.txt](192-first-reading.txt). It was not hashed before decoding, and the file was retyped from the session record after the working copy was deleted, so its being untouched rests on my word. [192-transcription.txt](192-transcription.txt) differs from it at 208 tokens. That figure is a sequence difference and includes renamed shapes and changed tokenization; it is not a count of corrected errors. Those changes are of three kinds: a sign re-read on a tighter crop of that sign, a shape given its own name (the closed-loop y, plain numerals), and places where blind readers gave a sign I had read differently or not at all, and I adopted theirs. In the first round that was about 27 signs in blotted stretches. In the second round it was most of the signs on thirteen lines that both readers gave alike, and after the Codex review the handful it pointed to and I confirmed on a crop. A change made only because a word needed it is not in the transcription. It is a row of the repairs file, or the word is left as an unread group with my gloss beside it.

Two fresh readers transcribed the same strips from a legend of shape names, with no key, no values and no other file ([blind/](blind/)). Each face has one first-round witness. Decoded with the same key, a blind sign or one of its alternatives gives the letters the reading prints at 1,478 of the 1,730 signs I count as read (85%), and at 1,533 (89%) if three consistent differences of naming are allowed for, such as their *d* for the sigma-like bare s. The count includes the 27 or so signs I adopted from these passes, which agree by construction.

A second round went back to the thirteen lines that held the unread clauses (R4–6, R10, R12, V1, V4–5, V8–10, V15–16), with tighter crops and a legend that spelled out how superscript 1 differs from 5 ([blind2/](blind2/)). Two more fresh readers worked separately, one in reverse line order. Where both gave the same sign and it differed from mine, I usually took theirs; in a few places I kept my own against both, among them the *nn* sign at R4 and *Borgogna* at V8. That is what opened V8–10, *venne nuova a .4. come quella Maestà* at V1, *le [co]se de[l] Re secure* at V5, *[f]ranzese* at R6 and *de' quali [mi parlò]* at R12. Readings resting on that agreement have two blind witnesses and no key-aware one, which is the opposite footing from the rest of the letter. The comparison script does not score this second round. The remaining tenth is mostly superscripts, above all 5 read as 1, which changes a vowel. Where the two disagree, my reading rests on the word, and that is a weaker footing than the page. The blind passes independently give *Martelli*, *Cales*, *Borbona*, *Tucti Sancti*, *credo*, *gratia*, *fine desiderato* and the figures 8, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 20 and 22. They give these as recognizable shapes, in several cases with a vowel or a double that differs from mine. They do not give 26.

## Documentary checks

Marino Sanudo's *Diarii*, vol. XVII, cols. 321–324, records a separate report of the king assigning Bourbon to Burgundy with **900 men-at-arms and 10,000 infantry**, later called Landsknechte. This confirms the commander, the destination and the infantry. The letter's 8 hundred lances and Sanudo's 900 men-at-arms are different figures and I have not reconciled them. Another report in the same volume gives 1,500 lances and 15,000 Landsknechte. The same volume prints a Florentine letter of 28 August 1513 that cites reports from *nostro Piero Martelli* at Vicenza, with the Spanish army then facing the Venetians (index: Martelli Pietro, cols. 25–26). The sign before *Martelli* at R7 would be his initial. A Martelli letter from that front reporting a rout, forwarded from Florence on 11 October, fits the Venetian defeat near Vicenza on 7 October, which Sanudo's volume covers at length around Creazzo and Olmo. This is the documentary ground for reading 15 as the Venetians. The volume also records that news of a defeat changed French plans to send troops to Italy, that the people of Tournai had sent to the French king to explain their conduct, and that prelates were to be sent to Rome. [Sanudo, vol. XVII](https://archive.org/details/idiariidimarinos17sanu)

Desjardins and Canestrini, *Négociations diplomatiques de la France avec la Toscane*, vol. II, print Acciaiuoli's dispatch from Amiens of 26 September 1513 from this filza. The next letter of his in the edition is from Blois, 27 January 1514. The letter of 23 October is not there. [Desjardins, vol. II](https://archive.org/details/gri_33125010469852)

**Acciaiuoli's key.** Pietro Domenico Gabbrielli's nineteenth-century compilation of cipher alphabets for the correspondence of the Dieci di Balia has, as **no. 120, Acciaioli (de) Roberto, 1512–14**, a reconstruction of this cipher from deciphered letters in eight filze of that series. DECODE holds the volume, and the key is its record R5807 (catalogued one leaf off, under the name of no. 121). My transcription is [gabbrielli-120.md](gabbrielli-120.md). It gives the single-letter alphabet, the syllabary, the doubles, 35 word signs, the nulls and thirteen numbers. It confirms the system and nearly every value used here, including the ones first worked out on this letter: the closed-loop y for o, the pi-like p, the hooked 9 for z, the gallows h, the two i signs, the looped phi for x beside the slashed circle for t, and D with a raised e for *come*. It supplied *quando* (which I had read as *se*), *quanto*, *ora*, *tanto* and *galee*, and it gives 22 as *Lanzichinecchi*. The letter of 23 October 1513 was not among Gabbrielli's sources, so the key is independent of it. Gabbrielli's two other keys for Acciaioli do not help with the open numbers, because they are different ciphers. No. 89 (1505–12, DECODE R5777) is an earlier system with its own signs and a nomenclator in which 26 is the lord of Faenza. No. 111 (1511–12, DECODE R5799) uses even numbers only, with 54 for the Swiss and 64 for the Venetians, against 20 for the Swiss here. Neither has a 15 or a 47, and their numbers cannot be carried over to this cipher. [DECODE record](https://de-crypt.org/decrypt-web/RecordsView/5807) · [Gabbrielli's index, image 2](https://dataverse.yale.edu/api/access/datafile/7983)

## Grades

Of the 1,730 signs accepted into the reading, 1,616 are read by values that stand in Gabbrielli's key no. 120, from the sign as transcribed. That key is a nineteenth-century archivist's reconstruction for this correspondent, made from letters deciphered at the time. It is independent attestation, but it is not a period key sheet, and I report it as "attested in Gabbrielli 120" and not as grade H without qualification. A further 11 are read by Dudout's values alone, 48 by values fixed on the 190/191 pair (C), and 15 by values that rest on sense within this letter. Seventeen are read at a sign marked doubtful (M) and 23 after a declared repair (I). The 157 unread signs are M. The percentage of signs measures what the editor's markup accepts: a sign inside an unread group is not counted, a sign outside one is, and the scripts do not judge where the groups are drawn.

By words: about 710 are read from the signs with nothing supplied, which includes words touched by a declared repair or a doubtful sign and both occurrences of the unidentified 15. About 11 more have a letter supplied inside them, about 14 are groups I gloss without a query, about 27 are groups I gloss with a query, and about 22 are unread, out of some 785. Read strictly, as Cyphersolver's rule asks (a word with a supplied letter is a hole), that is about 90%. Codex's stricter recount, which also sets aside repaired and doubtful words, gives 86%. Counting every bracketed supply would give about 97%, but a bracket is my proposal and not a reading.

## Reproducing the reading

From the repository root:

```sh
python3 amiens-1513/decode.py
python3 amiens-1513/verify.py
python3 amiens-1513/blind_compare.py
```

The first prints the literal decode of the 41 manuscript lines from the transcription, the key and the repairs. The second checks that [192-decode.txt](192-decode.txt) and every row of [192-reading.txt](192-reading.txt) follow from them letter for letter, recounts the signs by how they are read, counts the signs changed since the first reading, and exits non-zero if a committed file is stale. The third recomputes the agreement with the blind passes. These check derivation. Whether a sign on the page is the sign in the transcription has to be checked against the manuscript images, which are not redistributed here.

Andrew Aymeloglu's research project. OpenAI Codex made the first transcription and identified the key and the Bourbon passage. Claude re-transcribed both faces from the images, extended the key and made the reading above. Five further Claude instances made the four blind passes and the 190/191 calibration. Codex then reviewed the reading against the manuscript and key images; its report is [codex-review.md](codex-review.md). No model other than Claude has reviewed this reading.
