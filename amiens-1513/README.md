# Roberto Acciaiuoli at Amiens, 23 October 1513

**Read in large part, 20 November 2026.** Of the 1,890 cipher signs on the two faces, 1,660 (88%) are read as running Italian. The letter says that Louis XII had ordered Monsieur de Bourbon into Burgundy with lances and Landsknechte, and that a French descent into Italy had been in view. A copy of a Martelli letter, reporting the rout of the party written as 15, has overturned that plan. Acciaiuoli then reports a long conversation with the king, who is torn between fear of the Swiss and anger at people who excuse their conduct by fear of the English army. He follows the English king's embarkation at Calais, counts the French army that would meet a Swiss attack, and ends with the prelates leaving for Rome after All Saints and the king's wish to satisfy the pope. Two lines on the verso and a dozen shorter stretches are still unread, and three code numbers are identified from context only.

The manuscript is Florence, Archivio di Stato, Mediceo avanti il Principato, filza LXXII, doc. 192, old c. 234. The archive's images are 1272 × 1688 pixels, its native maximum. The heading is plural, *Magnifici viri*, and the subscription names Robertus Acciarolus. The cipher occupies 22 lines on the recto and 19 on the verso, the last ending where ordinary handwriting resumes. In the clear closing Acciaiuoli commends himself to the recipients and dates the letter *Ex Amiens, die xxiii Octobris M.D.XIII*, which gives the date independently of the cipher.

## What is still open

- **Unread signs.** 230 signs sit in stretches that decode to letters but not yet to words. The largest are R5 (the clause after the second *maraviglia*), R12 (after *contro a 26*), V1 (how the news of the English king arrived and where he was), V5, V9–10 and V15–16. They are marked in [192-reading.txt](192-reading.txt). At 1272 pixels the superscript figures in these places cannot be told apart reliably.
- **Code 15.** The party whose rout upset the plan, and whose backing a French descent would have had. I read it as the Venetians, routed at La Motta on 7 October 1513 while allied with France. This rests on context. No pair of cipher and clear fixes it.
- **Code 26.** The target of the king's anger. In the later Pandolfini letters 26 is the archduke. The speech that follows fits subjects who gave way to the English, and Sanudo reports that the people of Tournai sent to the king to explain their conduct. I leave 26 unresolved between these.
- **Code 22.** The troop category counted in thousands. Dudout tentatively reads *lanzichenech*, and Sanudo's report of the same order calls the infantry Landsknechte.
- ***Monsignore*** in the exchange about excuses is not named.
- **Word signs** R with a small circle (R10), B with a raised 1 (V15, V16), a large V (V15, V16) and a capital-H-like sign (R6) have no value yet. A lone triangle at R11 and R18 reads *m* everywhere else but makes no word there.
- **Numerals.** 8, 10, 12, 14 and the 2 and 5 on V6 are read as figures because *cento* or *mila* follows. An unmarked 8 is elsewhere the letter t, and 2 and 5 are elsewhere o and m.

## How it was read

Brice Dudout reconstructed a Florentine syllabary for Francesco Pandolfini's letters from the French court in 1514–15. A consonant sign takes its vowel from a superscript figure, 1–5 for **u, o, i, e, a**. There are bare vowel and consonant signs, doubled consonants, whole-word signs, and numbers used as code words. Acciaiuoli's letter of a year earlier uses the same system, and most of Dudout's values apply unchanged. [Dudout's key and source pairs](https://github.com/dbourdeau/cyphersolver/tree/main/targets/pandolfini1514)

[key.tsv](key.tsv) lists every value used here and where it comes from. Rows marked `v6` are Dudout's. Rows marked `192` are values this letter needed that his key lacks or writes differently: a tall 3 with an ascender for **a**, a closed-loop y for **o**, a g-like sign for **z**, a pi-like sign for **p**, a plain cross for **n**, a barred circle for **x**, a barred long s for **ss**, and the word signs for *se*, *vostre* and *nostro*. Each is attested by several words that read only that way, such as *inexpecttatamente*, *exercito*, *dixe* and *excusarsi* for x. The 2-like sign is c by default and b by sense, as in Dudout's material; the twenty places where I read b, a numeral or another departure from the sign as written are listed one by one in [192-repairs.tsv](192-repairs.tsv).

Two internal checks do not depend on the sense of a single passage. The sign sequence for *distribui-* occurs with two endings, *distribuire le gvernigione* (R2) and *distribuito el passag[i]o loro* (V4). *Gvernigione* recurs at R22, where the troops assigned at R2 have still not left.

**How far the transcription can be trusted.** I made [192-transcription.txt](192-transcription.txt) from enlarged strips with the key in hand, so it is not blind. Two fresh readers then transcribed the same strips with a legend of shape names and no key, no values and no other file ([blind/](blind/)). Token by token they agree with my pass on 1,489 of 1,913 paired signs (78%). Most of the 424 disagreements are of two kinds: the readers named a shape differently from me in a consistent way (the bare s sign, the tall a, the ll sign), or they read a superscript 5 as 1. The second kind matters, because it changes a vowel. Where my reading depends on such a superscript the word decides it, and that is a weaker footing than the page.

## Documentary checks

Marino Sanudo's *Diarii*, vol. XVII, cols. 321–324, records a separate report of the king assigning Bourbon to Burgundy with **900 men-at-arms and 10,000 infantry**, later called Landsknechte. This confirms the commander, the destination and the infantry. The letter's 8 hundred lances and Sanudo's 900 men-at-arms are different figures and I have not reconciled them. Another report in the same volume gives 1,500 lances and 15,000 Landsknechte. The volume also records that news of a defeat changed French plans to send troops to Italy, that the people of Tournai had sent to the French king to explain their conduct, and that prelates were to be sent to Rome. [Sanudo, vol. XVII](https://archive.org/details/idiariidimarinos17sanu)

Desjardins and Canestrini, *Négociations diplomatiques de la France avec la Toscane*, vol. II, print Acciaiuoli’s dispatch from Amiens of 26 September 1513 from this filza. The next letter of his in the edition is from Blois, 27 January 1514. The letter of 23 October is not in the edition. [Desjardins, vol. II](https://archive.org/details/gri_33125010469852)

The photographic index to Gabbrielli’s historical compilation lists **no. 120, Acciaioli Roberto, 1512, 1513, 1514**. The online Yale reel has volume I, with image files through 58-51 and three index images. The entry names a surviving key to compare. I have not obtained its table. It is the way to fix codes 15, 22 and 26 and the open word signs. [Gabbrielli's index, image 2](https://dataverse.yale.edu/api/access/datafile/7983) · [Yale reel inventory](https://doi.org/10.60600/YU/XKVOWE)

The public images of docs. 190–192 (Florence viewer IDs 79570–79575) were examined, including the back of 191. No clear copy of 192 was found among docs. 189 and 193–197.

## Grades

Under the repository's conventions the values taken from Dudout's key are grade C, since that key was fixed from pairs of cipher and contemporary clear. Of the 1,660 signs read, 1,627 are read by the key from the sign as transcribed, 13 at a sign I marked doubtful (M), and 20 after a declared repair (I). Among the 1,627, the signs whose value was first fixed on this letter (the `192` rows of the key) rest on sense within this text and not on a clear copy. The 230 unread signs are M. Words and letters in square brackets are I.

## Reproducing the reading

From the repository root:

```sh
python3 amiens-1513/decode.py
python3 amiens-1513/verify.py
```

The first prints the literal decode of the 41 manuscript lines from the transcription, the key and the repairs. The second checks that [192-decode.txt](192-decode.txt) and every row of [192-reading.txt](192-reading.txt) follow from them letter for letter, recounts the read and unread signs, and exits non-zero if any committed file is stale. It checks derivation. Whether a sign on the page is the sign in the transcription has to be checked against the manuscript images, which are not redistributed here.

Andrew Aymeloglu's research project. OpenAI Codex made the first transcription and identified the key and the Bourbon passage. Claude re-transcribed both faces from the images, extended the key and made the reading above; two further Claude instances made the blind passes.
